Aegislash: A Two-Pass Privacy Firewall for
Multilingual Indian Healthcare Records

Saket Kumar (leader), Ayush Aditya, Krishnendu Chakraborty, Krishna Nema

Team curl_fun  ·  Track 3, Secret Wards  ·  github.com/doublerook777/Aegislash

Abstract. Aegislash is a privacy firewall that judges identity and re-identification risk across the whole dataset before it writes a single file. It de-identifies English, Devanagari Hindi and Hinglish hospital records, offline on a CPU, with no language model. On the practice data, which guided the rules, 0 of 3,230 personal values remain visible, 99.6% of medical values are kept and linking F0.5 is 1.00. Detection on unseen text is weaker (Section 4).

1Introduction and unique features

Scrubbers that treat each string on its own fail on multi-hospital records in three ways: one name appears in two scripts and several spellings; identifiers hide in file names, headers, JSON keys and encoded attachments; and doctor names, doses such as 1-0-1 and complaints after C/O look like personal data. Our unique features are (i) context rules that protect clinical text, (ii) cross-script patient linkage that prefers a missed link to a wrong merge, and (iii) coarsening that is stronger only for patients in small or rare groups.

2System architecture

3Method

3.1Ingestion

Files are read in sorted order from UTF-8, UTF-16 or cp1252. Unsupported or broken files are quarantined, never copied. File names, headers, keys and export metadata are scanned too; base64 and escaped text are decoded.

3.2Detection

Column and key context is combined with patterns and checksums (Aadhaar Verhoeff, ABHA, PAN, phone, MRN), cue words in the three scripts (S/o, ji, उमर) and name lists. Guards keep clinical text: Dr. names, C/O complaints, doses, vitals and eponymous diseases.

3.3Linkage and pseudonyms

Devanagari names are transliterated; a consonant-skeleton key blocks candidates, and matching allows spelling variants. Records link on a strong ID, or on a name plus a second clue (date of birth, relative, or village with age ±1) that is unique in the dataset; conflicting clusters are split. Each linked patient receives six base-32 characters from a keyed hash, with secret s and patient key p:

pseudonym(p) = PSN- ‖ base32(HMAC-SHA256(s, "psn|" ‖ p))

3.4Privacy layer

All dates of one patient move by the same secret, non-zero offset of at most 180 days, taken from HMAC-SHA256(s, "shift|" ‖ p). Date formats and lengths of stay are preserved; an unlinked record gets its own offset. Patients are grouped by 5-year age band, gender and district, and assigned a tier (Table 1). A small group has fewer than 5 patients; a rare condition occurs in at most 3. Diagnoses are kept in every tier.

Unseen data. On text from an AI told not to read our code, scored before we inspected failures, detection covered 82% of personal values in free-text lines and 85% in exports (26 November). Across batches by different writers, coverage ranged from about 55% to 89%; later fixes have no new unseen score. On a separate file-name set, a given-name list raised names caught from 51 to 55 of 80 and cut false flags from 51 to 8 of 250.

5Privacy–utility trade-off

The run marked 622 of 818 records as generalised and 152 as suppressed. Patients in standard groups keep 5-year ages, district and a PIN prefix; those at higher risk lose location detail and age precision. Most of the 20 lost medical values are district names hidden on purpose. Coarsening is a single pass that does not guarantee k ≥ 5: 43 of 50 patients remain in groups smaller than 5. We accept this risk to keep diagnoses and stay lengths.

6Security design

We assume the server will one day be breached. A separate command, scripts/vault.py, makes an encrypted copy of the raw files: AES-256-GCM, a new key per file, wrapped by a master key kept outside data and code. File names are encrypted; altered, swapped or missing files are rejected. The pipeline writes no intermediate files, logs only counts and error codes, and never writes its secret. Unsafe files are quarantined. Not covered: removing the original files, a managed key service, and memory swapped to disk.

7Limitations

  • Re-identification: in a release of this size, uniqueness remains high (Table 2).
  • Detection: names or places with no label, capital or other clue can be missed (file-name set: 25 of 80).
  • Timelines: 43 unlinked records are shifted one by one, losing the intervals between them.
